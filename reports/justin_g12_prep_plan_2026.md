## Section 3.1 — The USACO question, resolved

Justin worked through the **Bronze and Silver curricula on usaco.guide in C++, by self-study**. He has not entered a USACO contest and holds no division placement.

That distinction matters, because usaco.guide is a **community-built training resource, not an official USACO product** — the site states plainly that completing its modules confers no certification or division standing. Divisions are determined solely by contest performance at usaco.org.

**What this means for the application:**

- ✅ Accurate: *"self-studied competitive programming through the Bronze and Silver USACO Guide curricula in C++"*
- ❌ Never write: *"USACO Silver Division"*, *"USACO Gold"*, or anything implying a placement or contest result

On its own, this is not an AIF line. Every serious CS applicant has studied something, and "I completed a syllabus" doesn't distinguish him. Its value is entirely as an **input** — and the conversion is straightforward.

**Where it should convert — in priority order:**

**1. CCC Senior, February 2027. ★★★** This is the highest-value competitive-programming outcome available to Justin this cycle, and it outranks USACO for his purposes for a specific reason: **the CCC is administered by Waterloo's own CEMC.** It is Waterloo's home contest, the one their AIF readers recognize instantly, and a top-25 Senior result earns an invitation to the Canadian Computing Olympiad — the most elite Canadian high-school CS credential there is. A summer of Bronze and Silver algorithm work is close to ideal preparation for it.

**2. USACO contests, January–February 2027. ★★** Register at usaco.org this fall and actually compete. Having finished the Bronze and Silver curricula he should place out of Bronze quickly, and USACO promotes in-contest when a competitor scores well early — so Bronze → Silver → Gold across the first two contests is plausible. A real Gold placement is a genuine, externally verified credential. Treat it as welcome upside, not as something to plan the application around.

**3. Euclid, April 2027. ★★** The math counterpart, also a Waterloo contest. Already in the plan.

> **Schedule correction:** the USACO season starts in **January, not December**. The 2025-26 season ran Jan 9–12, Jan 30–Feb 2, Feb 20–23, plus a proctored US Open on March 28; 2026-27 dates are not yet published. If the pattern holds, roughly two contests land before the Waterloo AIF, the third falls at or after it, and the US Open is upside only. **Registration and date-checking need to happen in the fall** — and note the first contest sits within days of the OUAC January 15 deadline, so that week will be busy.

**His best single artifact.** On July 20 he ran the voltage series on Hongjian's PhD project — an adhesive gel that cures underwater and releases irreversibly when current is passed through it. Justin tested 3, 5, 7 and 9 V and found **adhesion force falls exponentially with voltage, with complete detachment at 9 V.** The project has two live competing hypotheses for the mechanism: non-covalent gel–metal bonds breaking, versus an electrochemical reaction roughening both surfaces and reducing contact area.

**He also understands the physics.** The notes are not copied definitions. Two tells: he records that zeta potential is a *proxy* for double-layer repulsion measured at the slipping plane rather than the repulsion itself, and he explains why force is converted to stress (÷ bonded area) — to normalize out sample geometry so values compare across samples. Students write those things down only after they have understood why.

### How to frame it — and how not to

**This is soft-matter and colloid science in a Chemical & Materials Engineering department. It is not clinical research, and a BHSc reader will spot an overclaim instantly.**

The honest bridges are through **biomaterials and drug delivery**, and they are genuinely present in what he saw: chitosan/TPP nanoparticles (Tom's work, explicitly targeting antibacterial and antifungal application, and a well-established drug-delivery vehicle); self-healing alginate/PAAM hydrogels (a major tissue-engineering and wound-dressing class); sialic acid (a central cell-surface sugar); and electrically-releasable adhesives (an obvious fit for removable surgical adhesives). Zeng's group lists biomedical engineering among its areas.

The accurate claim — *he worked on the physical chemistry underlying biomaterials, and can explain why colloidal stability determines whether a drug-delivery particle works* — is both more impressive and more defensible than a vague gesture at medical research.

## Section 8 — What this analysis can and cannot tell you

The percentile figures come from Reddit-sourced self-reports, which skew heavily toward accepted applicants — roughly 95%+ of the sample. Every percentile here is Justin's standing **within the admitted population**, not his probability of admission. The real admission rates are far lower than this data implies. Treat the verdicts as floor estimates and relative rankings, not as odds.

Two further limits worth naming. The out-of-province samples are small (McMaster n=7, Queen's n=8) and the observed floors could move meaningfully with one or two more data points. And the 2026-27 supplementary application prompts at McMaster and Queen's will differ from 2025-26 — the format and rubric are stable year to year, the specific questions are not. Prepare for the shape of the questions, not for last year's wording.

What the data does support, consistently and across all four cycles: these programs are compressed at the top, small GPA movements near 96–98% cross large fractions of the distribution, and supplementary application quality separates candidates at the margin more than grades do. The ELA argument in Section 2.3 rests on the first of those and is robust to the caveats.
