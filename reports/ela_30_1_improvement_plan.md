## Part 1 — How the mark is actually built

### The final mark

| Component | Weight |
|---|---:|
| **School-awarded mark** | **70%** |
| Diploma exam | 30% |

### Inside the diploma exam

| Component | % of exam | % of final course mark |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Part B: Reading** — 70 machine-scored questions | 50% | 15% |
| **Part A, Assignment I: Personal Response to Texts** | 20% | 6% |
| **Part A, Assignment II: Critical/Analytical Response** | 30% | 9% |

**Assignment I** — Personal Response to Texts, 600–1200 words, ~45–60 min. Scored on Ideas and Impressions (10% of exam) + Presentation (10%).

**Assignment II** — Critical/Analytical Response, 800–1600 words, ~1½–2 hr. Scored on Thought and Understanding (7.5%), Supporting Evidence (7.5%), Form and Structure (5%), Matters of Choice (5%), Matters of Correctness (5%).

The scale is descriptive, not numeric: **Excellent (E) · Proficient (Pf) · Satisfactory (S) · Limited (L) · Poor (P)**, plus **Insufficient (INS)** — a special category scoring **zero across every category**, triggered by writing in a non-prose form, writing too little to assess, or not addressing the topic.

**Time: designed for 3 hours, but every student may take up to 6.** Universal, not an accommodation.

## Part 2 — Five conclusions that follow from that structure

### 1. The classroom mark is 70%. It is the main event.

Every essay written for class matters more than every practice diploma written at home. To hit 92–93% overall needs roughly a 93–94% school mark alongside a 90%+ diploma — a two-to-three point classroom improvement on ELA 20-1's 90%, not a transformation.

Week 1 of September, ask the ELA teacher **four** questions:

1. Which of the five Critical/Analytical categories is my weakest?
2. What separates a Proficient paper from an Excellent one in your marking?
3. Will you look at a thesis paragraph before I write the full essay?
4. **Can I get retired full-length Part B forms, and the class's item analysis — plus one extra form I won't look at until January?** Teachers can reach per-question provincial reports on the Extranet that students can't. This is the highest-value practice material available, it costs one question — and the sealed extra form becomes the mid-year re-measurement instrument (see Step 0).

Teachers answer these generously and almost nobody asks.

### 2. Part B (Reading) is his weakness — which makes it the top diploma target.

Part B is his identified weak area. It is also **50% of the diploma** (15% of the final course mark), objectively scored, and the most drillable component in the course. Those three facts together make it the highest-value target available.

**Why a STEM-strong reader struggles here.** Part B is not a comprehension test. Alberta assesses *critical reading and thinking; understanding of vocabulary; appreciation of tone, figurative language and rhetorical devices; understanding of the purposes and effects of writers' choices; appreciation of human experience and values*. Almost none of that is "what does the text say" — it is "**why did the writer do that, and what does it do to the reader.**" A reader trained on science and math extracts content accurately, then answers a question that was never about content. The mark is lost at the level of *reading mode*, not reading ability.

**"Best answer," not "right answer."** Two or three options are often defensible and one is *most* supported. The fix is mechanical: for every question, identify the specific words in the text that make the answer correct. If you can't point at them, it's the wrong answer.

**Some questions link two or more passages** by technique, context or theme. Synthesis needs separate practice.

**The blueprint confirms the diagnosis numerically.** Part B is built on a 3 × 3 grid — content dimension × cognitive dimension — and the item counts (verified against the bulletin's blueprint, p. 27) say where the marks live:

| Reporting category | A. Form literal understandings | B. Infer, apply, analyze | C. Assess and form generalizations | **Total** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1.** Construct meaning from content and context | | | | **30–40** |
| **2.** Relate forms, elements, techniques to content, purpose, effect | | | | **20–25** |
| **3.** Connect self, culture, milieu to text and creators | | | | **10–15** |
| **Total** | **5–15** | **30–40** | **15–25** | **70** |

Only **5–15 of 70 items are literal comprehension** — the mode a STEM-strong reader defaults to. Row 1 and column B are each up to 40 items, so inferring-and-analyzing-to-construct-meaning is where the exam concentrates. The marks aren't where the instinct goes.

*(Statistical note: the grid's cell-level counts aren't published — only these margins. Diagnose at the row/column level, which is well powered; treat any cell-level conclusion from a single practice set as provisional.)*

### 3. Proficient → Excellent is a specific, learnable distinction — and Alberta publishes the answer key.

| | Exploration of topic | Ideas | Support |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Proficient** | purposeful | thoughtful and considered | specific, *strengthens* |
| **Excellent** | **insightful** | **confident and discerning** | **precise, aptly reinforces** |

[*Examples of the Standards for Students' Writing*](https://www.alberta.ca/system/files/edc-ela-30-1-examples-of-students-writing.pdf) holds real student papers at each standard with marker commentary. **Highest-value study document in the course; most students never open it.** Read the Proficient and Excellent exemplars side by side in one sitting and write down what differs — reverse-engineering a marking standard from labelled examples.

### 4. Stop optimizing grammar. Start optimizing argument.

Matters of Correctness is **5%** of the exam. Thought and Understanding plus Supporting Evidence together are **15%** — three times as much. Justin's prose is already clean; extra polish on mechanics is near-worthless. Marginal hours belong in the quality of the *claim* and the *evidence*.

### 5. The Personal Response should be a strength.

Assignment I rewards genuine reflection, distinct voice, and an appropriate prose form — it explicitly permits forms beyond the five-paragraph essay. Justin has no shortage of usable personal material; the skill to practice is *selection and restraint*, not generation.

**Caution:** the response must genuinely address the given prompt and texts. A pre-written story bolted onto an unrelated prompt scores badly, and a non-prose form risks INS and a zero. Practice adapting *one true experience* to several different thematic prompts.

*Sequencing fix (Aug 5): the plan previously opened with a fully-justified practice set — which is training, not measurement. Once training starts, an uncontaminated baseline is no longer obtainable.*

**Take the 33 official [Released Items](https://www.alberta.ca/system/files/custom_downloaded_images/edc-diploma-exam-ela-30-1-released-items-2021.pdf) cold, in one sitting, answers only.** No justification, no lookups, no marking as you go. Record how long it took but do not impose a time limit.

**Why this document** *(contents verified directly against the PDF, Aug 6)*: it contains 33 Part B questions drawn from the June 2014/2015/2016/2017 and January 2016 forms, and its results table carries — per item — Alberta's **answer key, reporting-category code, plain-English item description, and the percentage of Alberta students who answered correctly**. That last column converts a bare score into a two-axis diagnostic:

| | Provincial % **high** (~75–85) | Provincial % **low** (~45–60) |
|---|---|---|
| **Got it right** | Baseline competence | Real strength |
| **Got it wrong** | **Fix first** — reading-mode or carelessness, not difficulty | Expected; the genuine skill ceiling |

Missing an item 83% of the province answered correctly is a completely different problem from missing one only 47% got — and a raw score cannot tell those apart. The cheap marks are the high-provincial-% misses; attack those first.

**Why untimed:** Part B is scheduled for 3 hours and every student may take up to 6, so speed is not a real constraint on exam day. A timed baseline would measure a pressure that doesn't exist and train exactly the rushing the method is designed to prevent.

**Interpretation caveats:**
- 33 items is noisy — treat the total score as a rough anchor. The durable outputs are the **row/column accuracy profile** and the **high-%-miss list**. (33 items across 9 blueprint cells is ~4 per cell — cell-level conclusions from one set are mostly noise; use the margins.)
- The provincial % figures date from 2014–2017 administrations — a sound difficulty proxy, not a precise present-day benchmark.
- The items must be spent **once, cold**: this is the *only* material with provincial comparison data, so using it for training burns the measurement instrument. Baseline and training must draw on different sources.

**Re-measurement (resolved Aug 6):** the January re-measure uses the **sealed retired form from the teacher** (question 4 in §1) — unseen since September, taken cold under the same protocol, with the Extranet item analysis playing the role of the provincial % column.

## Part 4 — The Part B method

*The training that runs after the baseline, aimed at whatever the baseline exposed.*

1. **After every question, write the line that proves the answer** — and one sentence per wrong option on why it fails. Roughly triples time per set and is worth every minute. Highest-yield technique available.
2. **Learn the distractor patterns** — too extreme; true of the passage but irrelevant to the question; true of the wrong section; a plausible inference the text doesn't support. Naming the pattern stops the trap.
3. **Categorize every error using Alberta's own blueprint taxonomy** (the 3×3 grid in §2) — the released items are already tagged with it, so sorting errors any other way throws away free labelling. Diagnose at row/column level.
4. **Paraphrase poetry into plain prose before answering.** Most poetry errors are failures to establish the literal sense first — not interpretive failures.
5. **Read literature between sets.** Poetry especially, and pre-twentieth-century prose. Fifteen minutes, Mon/Wed/Fri, asking *what is this doing and how* rather than *what does it mean*. September to June is enough runway if it starts now.

**Use the time.** 70 questions across 6 hours is more than five minutes per question. Being right beats being fast, and most students never use the extra time because nobody tells them it's universal.

## Part 6 — The admissions timing insight

**Confirmed: ELA 30-1 is a full-year course, September 2026 – June 2027, diploma in June 2027.** No January sitting — which means ten uninterrupted months of Part B runway, the best possible structure for the one component that rewards patient accumulation.

Ontario universities make conditional offers on **interim marks**:

- What universities see in **December and February is pure classroom work** — no diploma component at all.
- The final blended mark (70/30) does not exist until **July 2027**, after every decision in the cycle.

> **The diploma exam has almost no influence on whether Justin gets in. It determines whether the offer he already holds survives.** The admission decision runs entirely on classroom work between September and February.

**Practical resolution:** run Part B all year regardless — it's the weakness, and weaknesses need runway. But when a class assignment and diploma practice compete for the same evening before February, **the class assignment wins.**

## Part 7 — The plan

### August 2026 — baseline first

*Scheduling note: this collides with the final week of both summer placements and the Aug 13 closeout list. The rule: **if anything slips this week, the ELA items move — the Aug 13 items can't.***

1. **Select the literary texts** (~Aug 5) — two or three known deeply enough to bend to almost any prompt; ideally one Shakespeare, one novel, one shorter text.
2. **Read the Standards exemplars** (~Aug 7) — one sitting, Proficient vs Excellent side by side. Part A work, independent of the Part B track.
3. ✅ **Baseline — the 33 released items, cold, one sitting** (~Aug 8, protected block). Untimed, duration recorded. **Result: 92%.**
4. **One Personal Response** to a real released prompt, 45–60 min (~Aug 9, protected).
5. ✅ **Score and profile the baseline** (~Aug 10) — per miss, record the reporting-category code and provincial %. Outputs: row/column accuracy profile + the high-provincial-%-miss list.
6. ✅ **One training set, fully justified** (~Aug 14) — from the practice platform, targeted at the weakest **row or column** the baseline exposed. **Result: Part B practice 95%; Part A practice 84.**
7. ✅ **Poetry reading starts** — 15 min, Mon/Wed/Fri, ongoing since Aug 14.

### September–February — the classroom is what admissions sees

- Every class essay treated as a graded diploma paper: draft → feedback → revise. This is the 70%, and the only thing universities see before decisions.
- **One Part B set per week**, justified and categorized. Non-negotiable.
- One full Part A every three weeks, timed, scored against the official categories.
- Standing feedback loop with the teacher — thesis paragraphs before full drafts; the four §1 questions in week 1.
- When classwork and diploma practice collide, classwork wins.
- **January: re-measure** with the sealed teacher form, cold, same protocol as the baseline.

### March–June — the diploma push

- Part B to two sets a week; target the error categories that persist.
- Full timed Part A monthly, biweekly from April.
- **Rehearse the long sitting** — practise at a 4–5 hour pace at least twice.

### Marking his own practice

Score every practice paper against the five Critical/Analytical categories **separately** — never a single overall impression. The point is to find out whether marks are lost in Thought and Understanding (expensive) or Matters of Choice (cheap). A global grade hides that, and hiding it is how months of practice produce no improvement.
